## Corpus signals

Original chapters 1–43:

- about 270,600 Korean characters
- average chapter length about 6,300 characters
- average paragraph length about 40 characters
- average sentence length about 22 characters
- dialogue paragraphs about 38 percent
- short reaction or transition paragraphs about 22 percent

March revision prologue–chapter 6:

- about 37,800 characters
- average chapter length about 5,400 characters
- denser paragraphs, less dialogue, and more deliberate action presentation

Use these values as warning bands, not quotas. Scene purpose overrides numeric
matching.

For spring prose, apply this profile together with
[narration-style-card.md](narration-style-card.md) and the appearing-character
entries in [voice-cards-spring.md](voice-cards-spring.md). This profile and the
approved style anchor remain higher authority. Card examples are `STYLE_ONLY`
and do not authorize events.

## Core movement

Build the prose from:

1. concrete action or visible condition
2. short reaction
3. only the explanation needed to understand the consequence

Alternate two to four narrative paragraphs with dialogue, physical response,
or observation. Do not make every paragraph a fragment.

## Narration

- Use accessible Korean web-serial prose and concrete verbs.
- Use scene-focused third person. Keep one perceptual center per scene.
- Let the reader see the action before naming the emotion.
- Show emotion first through gaze, hands, posture, food, chores, play,
  hesitation, or a failed ordinary action.
- Use present tense selectively to accelerate immediate action or observation.
  Keep surrounding tense controlled; do not reproduce accidental source drift.
- Place exposition after a question, action, discovery, or decision that makes
  the information necessary.
- Keep the super-robot scale and a child's immediate daily concern visible in
  the same scene.

## Dialogue voices

### Do Dana

- Short, blunt, physical, and immediate.
- React through food, exercise, annoyance, embarrassment, unfairness, or the
  next action.
- Interpret unfamiliar ideas literally rather than pretending expertise.
- Express resolve close to action, not as a polished thematic speech.

### Do Sua

- Calmer, observant, and syntactically more complete than Dana.
- Correct Dana quietly and summarize observed facts.
- Use polite language with adults.
- Let childlike wants surface around snacks, play, praise, or exclusion.
- In combat, verbalize position, sequence, timing, and tool use.

### Do Young-jin

- Clipped, stern, and sparing with explanations.
- Express care through repair, preparation, food, clothing, transport, and
  protection rather than emotional speeches.
- State technical matters as problem and action, not a lecture.

### Friends and adults

- Build school banter from teasing, literal answers, exaggeration, nicknames,
  and quick corrections.
- Mix institutional terms with dry everyday speech in adult or military scenes.
- Do not give every character the same wit, vocabulary, or sentence length.

## Everyday scenes

1. Start with a small desire, inconvenience, task, or promise.
2. Reveal the relationship through talk and hand-level action.
3. Embed shortage, repair economy, weather, school life, or neighborhood
   texture inside the practical problem.
4. Let one threat clock enter lightly: bodily symptoms, a lie, a friend's
   suspicion, military alertness, or a monster signal.
5. End with at least one changed relationship, knowledge state, active clue, or
   living condition.

Pure daily-life chapters are allowed. Unchanged chapters are not.

## Combat scenes

- Establish position, distance, direction, and contact point.
- Describe preparation and result before decorative attack naming: plant,
  brace, grab, turn, drag, strike, throw.
- Alternate short impact paragraphs with tactical interpretation.
- Let Dana solve through boxing, taekwondo, balance, and bodily familiarity.
- Let Sua solve through observation, timing, terrain, and tool use.
- Give each attack one primary consequence and connect combinations through
  visible causality.
- Reserve onomatopoeia for decisive impacts.
- Change combat grammar by season and current equipment. Never borrow an old
  weapon state.

## Humor

- Create humor from a mismatch in scale or concern.
- A giant robot secret may lose to lunch, homework, allowance, heat, chores, or
  a broken object.
- Let bureaucratic frustration and adult understatement supply dry humor.
- Do not explain the joke after it lands.
- Let humor help characters endure seriousness without mocking the stakes.

## Chapter endings

- Prefer a choice, object, stopped hand, alarm, unfinished lie, glance, physical
  symptom, or ironic ordinary act.
- Let the final concrete image change the meaning of the preceding paragraph.
- Avoid abstract thematic summaries and generic foreboding.
- Not every chapter needs a threat cliffhanger; an emotional afterimage or
  small expectation is enough.

## Do not imitate

- spelling, spacing, punctuation, and quotation-mark errors in the old text
- accidental head-hopping or uncontrolled tense mixing
- explaining an emotion after action has already shown it
- overgrown military or intelligence pursuit subplots
- artificial one-line-paragraph spam
- generic phrases such as unexplained destiny, ineffable feelings, or vague
  wheels of fate
- uniformly clever dialogue
- ornamental literary metaphors that slow a practical scene
